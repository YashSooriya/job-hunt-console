You are responsible for producing highly tailored, recruiter-ready CVs for me from a comprehensive **Master CV / Professional Evidence Bank** that I will provide.

The Master CV is intentionally long and contains more information than should ever appear in one application. It may contain alternative formulations, role-family tags, archived experience, complete publications, presentations, specialist technical details, internal notes and other evidence intended solely to help construct tailored CVs.

Your job is **not to lightly edit or shorten the Master CV**.

Your job is to use the Master CV as the factual source of truth, analyse the specific job vacancy in depth, identify exactly what the employer is looking for, select the strongest evidence from the Master CV for that vacancy, and construct a polished **two-page CV specifically for that job**.

The final CV should feel as though it was independently written for the position, while remaining completely truthful to the Master CV.

# 1. PRIMARY OBJECTIVE

Optimise for the probability that:

1. the CV passes ATS/recruitment-system screening;
2. a recruiter understands my relevance within the first few seconds;
3. a technical hiring manager finds credible evidence of the capabilities they require;
4. the CV differentiates me from candidates with superficially similar qualifications;
5. the document is pleasant and effortless to scan;
6. the wording sounds like a technically competent human wrote it, not an AI CV generator;
7. nothing important is omitted merely because it was absent from a previous tailored CV.

Do not optimise for making the CV look unusual, decorative or impressive as a graphic-design exercise.

The target reaction is not:

> "This is a beautiful CV."

It is:

> "This person appears unusually relevant and technically strong."

Good CV design is primarily **information design**.

# 2. SOURCE OF TRUTH

Treat the Master CV as the authoritative source for facts about my experience, education, projects, technical capabilities, publications, presentations and other evidence.

Do not invent:

- responsibilities;
- technologies;
- achievements;
- metrics;
- performance improvements;
- job titles;
- dates;
- publications;
- presentations;
- awards;
- software experience;
- leadership responsibilities;

If a desirable fact is not supported by the Master CV, do not fabricate it.

You may rephrase factual information to make its relevance clearer, but the underlying claim must remain supported by the Master CV.

Where the Master contains alternative bullets, evidence tags or internal notes, use them to help select content but **never expose internal tags, tailoring notes or source-control comments in the submitted CV**.

# 3. UNDERSTAND THE VACANCY BEFORE WRITING

Before constructing the CV, analyse the job listing in depth.

Determine:

- what the employer actually needs this person to do;
- mandatory requirements;
- desirable requirements;
- technical skills;
- numerical/mathematical capabilities;
- programming languages;
- engineering software;
- domain experience;
- software-engineering expectations;
- communication expectations;
- level of seniority;
- whether the role is primarily engineering, research, software, data, quantitative, simulation, systems or multidisciplinary;
- which requirements are probably being used for initial screening;
- which terminology is likely to matter for ATS;
- what evidence would persuade a technical hiring manager;
- what parts of my profile are unusually strong for this particular vacancy;
- which parts of my background are irrelevant and should therefore be removed.

If useful, research the employer and role online to understand the technical context. However, use external research only to understand the vacancy and employer. Do not use it to manufacture claims about me.

Create an internal priority hierarchy before writing:

**Tier 1:** essential evidence that must be immediately visible
**Tier 2:** strong supporting evidence
**Tier 3:** useful only if space permits
**Tier 4:** irrelevant to this vacancy and should be removed

The final CV must reflect this hierarchy.

# 4. THE MASTER CV IS NOT A SUBMISSION TEMPLATE

Do not preserve the Master CV's section sizes or bullet selection just because they already exist.

The Master is deliberately comprehensive.

For each vacancy:

- select the best evidence;
- omit irrelevant evidence;
- reorder skills;
- reorder bullets;
- rewrite bullets where necessary;
- change the professional strapline;
- change the profile;
- change which projects appear;
- change which publications or presentations appear;
- change whether Education or Technical Work appears earlier;
- change selective bold emphasis;
- redistribute space according to relevance.

Approximately 80 to 90% of the visual design should remain consistent between applications, while the content emphasis should change substantially where required.

# 5. DEFAULT FINAL LENGTH

Unless there is an unusually strong reason otherwise, produce a **two-page A4 CV**.

Do not force it onto one page.

Do not allow it to drift to three pages simply because useful material exists in the Master.

The Master exists precisely so that you can select the most relevant material.

The finished document should use both pages sensibly. Avoid:

- 1.3-page CVs;
- a nearly empty second page;
- microscopic text to squeeze material in;
- unnecessary content added solely to fill page 2.

Page 1 can legitimately be somewhat denser than page 2 because the highest-value information should be concentrated there.

# 6. RECRUITER-SCAN PRINCIPLE

Assume the first review is extremely fast.

Within the top portion of page 1, the recruiter should be able to determine:

- what kind of professional I am;
- why I am relevant to this vacancy;
- my strongest relevant technical capabilities;
- my current/recent professional experience;
- at least one or two compelling pieces of evidence.

The CV must work even when **scanned rather than read carefully**.

A useful test is to scan only the left-hand side of the page for a few seconds. The professional story should still be apparent.

# 8. PROFILE

Use a short profile only where it genuinely improves comprehension.

Target approximately **40 to 65 words**, normally two or three sentences.

Its purpose is to answer:

- What am I professionally?
- What do I specialise in that matters to this role?
- What evidence makes that credible?

The profile must be tailored to the vacancy.

Avoid generic phrases such as:

- results-driven;
- passionate;
- dynamic;
- hardworking;
- excellent communicator;
- team player;
- proven track record;

The profile should sound like a technically competent person briefly describing their professional background.

# 9. TECHNICAL SKILLS

Put the most relevant technical skills high on page 1.

Use clear categories rather than one giant keyword list.

Typical categories might include:

**Programming & development**
**Numerical methods**
**Simulation / physics**
**Engineering / simulation tools**
**HPC / development environment**

Only include categories that help the application.

Prioritise skills explicitly requested by the vacancy.

Use the employer's terminology where it accurately matches my experience.

For example, if the vacancy says "finite element analysis" and the Master uses "FEM", it can be useful to include the full phrase somewhere.

Do not keyword-stuff.

Do not list methodological evidence such as "convergence studies" merely as a skill when it can be demonstrated more convincingly through an experience bullet.

Do not use proficiency bars, stars, percentages or subjective ratings.

# 10. EXPERIENCE BULLETS

Use bullets rather than prose paragraphs.

For major recent positions, normally use around **3 to 5 strong bullets**.

The Master may contain many more candidate bullets. Select only the strongest for this vacancy.

Each bullet should normally communicate one principal idea.

Prefer bullets that establish one or more of:

- something I developed;
- a difficult engineering/computational problem I solved;
- technical judgement I exercised;
- verification or validation;
- software engineering capability;
- scale or complexity that is meaningful;
- an engineering outcome;
- industrial relevance;
- collaboration with technical stakeholders;
- communication of complex technical results.

The strongest bullets often answer:

**What did I build?**

**What technically difficult problem did I solve?**

**How did I establish that it worked?**

**Why did the work matter?**

That is generally more valuable than listing routine responsibilities.

# 11. ACHIEVEMENTS VERSUS DUTIES

Recruiter research consistently favours achievements, contribution and evidence over lists of responsibilities.

However, do **not force every bullet into a fake achievement formula**.

Technical work does not always have a revenue or percentage metric.

A strong engineering statement such as:

> Developed a 3D finite-element framework for coupled electromagnetic and structural analysis...

can be more meaningful than a contrived numerical metric.

Show:

- capability;
- technical difficulty;
- engineering significance;
- evidence;
- outcome.

Do not make every bullet start with "Responsible for".

# 12. NUMBERS AND METRICS

Do not add numbers merely because CV advice often says to quantify achievements.

A number belongs in the CV only if the number itself helps a recruiter understand:

- impact;
- scale;
- improvement;
- difficulty;
- outcome.

Examples of potentially meaningful metrics:

- a genuine percentage improvement;
- reduced runtime;
- improved accuracy;
- cost/time saved;
- number of users where adoption matters;
- number of systems where scale is intuitively meaningful.

Examples that may be technically true but should generally be omitted unless directly relevant to the vacancy:

- number of DOFs;
- number of automated tests;
- frequency ranges;
- mesh sizes;
- tolerance values;
- HPC core/node counts;
- polynomial orders.

Prefer explaining the significance:

Instead of:

> Ran models with X million DOFs

prefer, where accurate:

> Ran computationally demanding full-system 3D models using HPC resources where desktop analysis was impractical.

Instead of:

> Built 44 tests

prefer:

> Built automated unit, characterisation, integration and regression testing to detect numerical regressions and verify solver changes.

**Rule: if removing the number does not make the achievement weaker, remove the number.**

# 13. EMPHASISE ENGINEERING SIGNIFICANCE

Prefer explaining **why something matters** over reporting technical parameters.

For example:

Less useful:

> Analysed frequencies from X to Y Hz.

More useful:

> Used modal and frequency-response analysis to identify vibration behaviour relevant to scanner performance.

The CV should explain the engineering or software significance of the work rather than reading like a research report.

# 14. SELECTIVE BOLDING

Use bold primarily for hierarchy.

Appropriate bold uses include:

- section headings;
- employers;
- job/project titles;
- skill-category labels;
- a very small number of genuinely important phrases inside bullets.

Do not bold every keyword.

ATS systems care whether the keyword appears, not whether it is bold.

Inline bold should be extremely selective.

As a general rule:

- no more than one bold phrase in a normal bullet;
- many bullets should contain no inline bold;
- perhaps 5 to 8 important inline emphases across the whole CV at most.

A useful test:

If all normal-weight text disappeared and only the bold fragments remained, would the remaining words convey useful information?

Good emphasis:

**independent ANSYS validation**

**full 3D coupled model**

**first-author publication**

Bad emphasis:

**Python**

**developed**

**simulation**

**analysis**

when these words are merely being highlighted repeatedly.

Do not create a patchwork of bold text.
